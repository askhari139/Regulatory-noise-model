\title{Regulatory stochasticity drives opposing phenotypic outcomes in cell-fate decision networks\\

}

\author{Abhay Gupta, Lakshmi Malvadi, Prakash Kulkarni, Ravi Salgia, Herbert Levine, \\ Mohit Kumar Jolly, Kishore Hari}

\begin{abstract}
Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) underlying cell-fate decisions are conventionally modelled as deterministic systems with fixed interaction parameters, yet the effective strength of any regulatory interaction fluctuates continuously in vivo. Intrinsically disordered regions in transcription factors, non-specific genomic binding sites, and stochastic enhancer dynamics collectively introduce temporal variability in the fold-change parameters that govern regulatory efficacy --- a regulatory noise that is distinct from, and largely independent of, classical copy-number noise. How such regulatory noise shapes the emergent phenotypic landscape of cell-fate circuits remains poorly understood. Here, we systematically evaluate the effects of three modes of regulatory noise --- additive (state-independent), multiplicative (state-dependent), and stationary (mean-reverting) --- on GRN motifs underlying cell-fate decisions. Using RACIPE-generated parameter ensembles, we simulate toggle switches, toggle switches with self-activation, and toggle triads under each noise mode across a wide range of noise magnitudes, and extend the analysis to larger cell-fate networks governing epithelial--mesenchymal transition and gonadal cell-fate determination, all using a Boolean framework. Additive noise consistently drives networks away from terminal attractors toward hybrid co-expression states, with the effect scaling with noise intensity and being strongest in bistable parameter regimes and sparser network architectures. Multiplicative noise, by contrast, reinforces inhibitory interactions over time and stabilises terminal phenotypes across all topologies studied. Stationary noise produces minimal disruption to the phenotypic landscape, consistent with a normal physiological baseline. These results establish that the type of regulatory noise, not only its magnitude, is a key determinant of phenotypic plasticity in cell-fate circuits, and suggest that additive-like noise --- plausibly arising from IDR conformational dynamics or open chromatin in stem cells --- may be a non-genetic driver of hybrid state enrichment and intra-tumour heterogeneity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:Intro}

GRNs are biological regulatory circuits that constitute a complex collection of molecular regulators including transcription factors (TFs), signaling molecules, and DNA segments that interact to regulate gene expression. They determine the spatiotemporal patterns of gene expression involved in determining cellular development, identity, and environmental responses. They also play a central role in determining the organism’s body plan, which is central to evolutionary developmental biology \cite{Davidson2005, Davidson2006, Maizels2026}. GRNs are conventionally modelled as deterministic systems with fixed interaction parameters, although it is clear that the effective strength of any regulatory interaction is subject to continuous fluctuation in vivo. For example, an overwhelming fraction of TFs are intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) with significant disordered regions . These disordered regions mediate conformationally heterogeneous, post-translational modification (PTM)-sensitive contacts with DNA and co-factors, causing regulatory affinities to vary dynamically rather than assume a fixed value \cite{Ferrie2022Structure-functionRegulation, Holehouse2023TheRegions}. Considering that 80-90\% of TFs are IDPs \cite{Staby2017}, understanding IDP conformational noise is paramount for understanding cell-fate specification. Although the individual conformers in an IDP ensemble are in fast exchange, certain conformations are stabilized by specific PTMs, especially phosphorylation, that exist with half-lives that range from minutes to hours contributing to ‘conformational noise’ \cite{Kulkarni2022, Kulkarni2019}. Non-specific TF binding to genomic decoy sites further modulates the effective regulatory signal in an affinity- and abundance-dependent manner \cite{Dey2020EnhancementSites, Burger2012InfluenceExpression}. Enhancer dynamics and transcriptional bursting add a third layer, coupling stochastic TF occupancy at cis-regulatory elements directly to fluctuations in target gene activation \cite{Raj2006StochasticCells, Fukaya2016EnhancerBursting}. The availability of TF binding sites can also vary over the course of transcription, due to noise in chromatin folding states \cite{Gamble2025}. Collectively, these mechanisms argue that the parameters governing regulatory interactions — rather than being static — are themselves stochastic quantities, and the consequences of such ''regulatory noise", i.e., temporal stochasticity in regulatory interaction parameters, for the dynamics of multistable GRN motifs underlying cell-fate decisions remain poorly understood.


This question is especially pressing in the context of cancer, where genetic instability, aberrant chromatin remodelling, and rewired signalling cascades collectively amplify both intrinsic gene expression noise and extrinsic cell-to-cell variability \cite{Brock2009Non-geneticTumours, Landau2014LocallyLeukemia, Hanahan2026HallmarksBeyond}. Non-genetic heterogeneity — stable phenotypic variation within a clonal population arising from gene expression noise and network multi-stability — is now recognised as a major contributor to drug tolerance and tumour progression \cite{Hanahan2026HallmarksBeyond, Franca2024CellularContinuum, Shaffer2017RareResistance}. In this setting, the parameters of regulatory interactions which govern the dynamics of non-genetic heterogeneity cannot be considered fixed: PTM landscapes, co-factor availability, and chromatin accessibility to TFs all fluctuate, effectively introducing stochasticity into the fold-change parameters that govern network behaviour. 


A natural starting point for studying how regulatory noise propagates through GRNs is the toggle switch — a simple mutual repression circuit between two master regulators that is among the most commonly occurring and extensively characterized motifs for binary cell-fate decisions \cite{gardner2000construction}. The toggle switch exhibits bistability under a broad range of kinetic parameters, and noise-induced switching between its two attractors has been studied in detail in both synthetic and natural contexts \cite{tian2006stochastic}. Many biological decisions, however, are not binary: T helper cell differentiation, haematopoietic lineage commitment, and epithelial–mesenchymal–hybrid state transitions all involve three or more stable phenotypes. Addition of self-activation to the toggle switch – TSSA (toggle switch with self-activation) stabilizes the intermediate phenotype – corresponding to hybrid phenotypes in EMT and progenitor states in differentiation cascades – where both nodes are expressed simultaneously \cite{Lu2013}. Three nodes connected to each other via a toggle switch, forming the toggle triad (TT) enables three distinct cell states, each defined by one node being highly expressed and the other two repressed \cite{doi:10.1098/rsif.2020.0631}. We will have occasion to study the 4-node toggle tetramer (see below). Importantly, although GRNs underlying cell-fate decision systems often involve a larger number of TFs than accounted for in these motifs, TFs corresponding to a phenotype often form teams such that nodes belonging to the same team activate each other. These teams mutually inhibit each other, thereby forming higher order forms of the motifs listed above. These higher order motifs have similar qualitative behavior as their small motif counterparts such as a two team network promoting bistability with a small fraction of intermediate states, similar to TSSA. 


The effect of stochasticity on toggle switch dynamics has been studied extensively, though almost exclusively through noise applied to molecular copy numbers rather than interaction parameters. Early work established that intrinsic noise — arising from the discreteness of protein and mRNA molecules — can drive spontaneous switching between the attractors of a multistable circuit, with switching rates that decrease exponentially with copy number and increase with noise magnitude \cite{tian2006stochastic, Wang2007Noise-inducedSwitch}. Analytical treatments using the Chemical Langevin Equation and mean first-passage time theory have characterized how the depth and asymmetry of the potential wells governing each attractor determine residence times and transition rates \cite{Wang2010TheDifferentiation, Strasser2012StabilityExpression, Xu2014SwitchSwitch}. A key finding across these studies is that noise type — not only noise magnitude — shapes attractor occupancy: additive and multiplicative noise applied to the same deterministic system produce different effective potential landscapes and therefore different stationary probability distributions \cite{Jaruszewicz2013ToggleGene}. Extending beyond Gaussian noise, Lévy noise models have shown that heavy-tailed fluctuations can induce coherent switching at substantially lower intensities than Gaussian noise requires, by enabling large jumps that entirely overcome the potential barrier \cite{Xu2016TheNoise}. At the network level, toggle switch dynamics have been studied under coupled and multicellular settings, where intracellular multiplicative noise can induce synchronised switching across cell populations via quorum-sensing pathways \cite{Wang2007Noise-inducedSwitch}. More recently, the sRACIPE framework demonstrated that expression-level (state) noise merges bistable clusters at high intensity, while static parametric variation across the model ensemble instead broadens clusters without destabilizing them \cite{Kohar2018RoleCircuits}. In both cases, however, the kinetic parameters themselves remain fixed once sampled; neither treats the parameter as a time-evolving stochastic process, which is the gap that our regulatory noise framework addresses. Stochastic analysis of the toggle triad and related tristable motifs has received comparatively little attention, with existing studies focused primarily on deterministic bifurcation structure and attractor geometry \cite{doi:10.1098/rsif.2020.0631, Duddu2025DistinctNetworks} rather than noise-driven state transitions. Whether the richer transition topology of the toggle triad — permitting direct switching between all three attractor pairs — confers qualitatively different susceptibility to regulatory noise compared to the toggle switch remains an open question.


In this manuscript, we study how regulatory noise – that is, the noise of parameters governing the interactions in GRNs – shapes the dynamics of these motifs systematically. We focus on the fold-change parameter \cite{Huang2017}, which describes the change in the production rate of a target node due to an upstream transcription factor. We couple stochastic fold-change perturbations with the Random Circuit Perturbation (RACIPE) framework \cite{Huang2017}. RACIPE generates large ensembles of ODE models with randomly sampled kinetic parameters, enabling topology-level inferences that are robust to parameter uncertainty. By superimposing continuous stochastic noise on the fold-change parameters of these ensembles, we successfully isolated the effect of regulatory noise from parameter-set-specific idiosyncrasies and characterized how mean residence times and switching frequencies depend on noise magnitude, noise mode, and attractor stability class. Our results reveal that regulatory noise is sufficient to induce state transitions that are inaccessible in the deterministic limit, and that bistable and tristable parameter regimes differ qualitatively in their susceptibility to such noise — with implications for understanding phenotypic plasticity in both normal development and disease.

\subsection{Simulating regulatory noise in GRN motifs underlying cell-fate decision}

We begin by analyzing the binary and tertiary cell-fate decision motifs --- the toggle switch (TS) and the toggle triad (TT) and their extensions that include self-activation edges (TSSA and TTSA). A toggle switch consists of two mutually inhibiting transcription factors (Figure~\ref{fig:1}A(i-ii)), whereas a toggle triad consists of three mutually inhibiting transcription factors (Figure~\ref{fig:1}A(iii-iv)). TS represents the core motif underlying binary cell-fate decision networks. The phenotypic landscape emergent from TS is dominated by two states with mutually exclusive expression patterns - either node has low expression while the other is high - $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$. Under appropriate parametric conditions, the toggle switch also exhibits bistability, enabling transitions between $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ states \cite{gardner2000construction}. The addition of self-activation (TSSA) stabilizes the $(1,1)$ state in which both nodes are co-expressed. This co-expression state, often referred to as a "hybrid" state has been associated with stemness in development \cite{Roeder2006TowardsPU.1} and with high plasticity and pro-metastatic features in cancer \cite{Pastushenko2019EMTMetastasis}.

While binary cell-fate decision systems dominate biology owing to their stability and robustness to internal and external perturbations \cite{harlapur2022functional, Shyam2025MutuallyNetworks}, some instances of tertiary cell-fate systems --- such as T-cell differentiation --- employ a toggle triad architecture. A toggle triad consists of three mutually inhibiting transcription factors (Figure~\ref{fig:1}A(iii)) and permits three differentiated, single-high states where one of the three nodes has a high expression while the other two have low expression ($(0,0,1)$, $(1,0,0)$, and $(0,1,0)$) as well as three hybrid, double-high states with two out of three nodes having high expression (e.g.\ $(1,1,0)$). Parameter sweeps of the toggle triad result predominantly in monostable and bistable configurations, with a relatively smaller fraction exhibiting tristability. Single network architectures that allow the co-existence of four or more mutually exclusive states are not currently known.

We model these networks using a Hill-equation kinetics-based ODE system (Equation~\ref{eq:ode}, \ref{eq:hill}, Section~\ref{sec:RACIPE}). 
\begin{equation}
\frac{dX}{dt} = g_X \prod_{i \in P} \frac{H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i})}{\lambda_{Y_i}} \prod_{i \in N} H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i}) - d_X X
\label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i}) = \lambda_{Y_i} \frac{Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}}{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}} + Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}} + \frac{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}}}{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}} + Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}}
\label{eq:hill}
\end{equation}
Briefly, these equations combine transcription and translation into a single production term and a first-order degradation term representing mRNA and protein turnover. The production term consists of a rate constant ($g_X$) representing polymerization of RNA and protein, together with a combination of shifted Hill functions that formalize transcriptional regulation by input nodes. Input nodes change the production rate via the fold-change parameter $\lambda$: for inhibitory inputs, $\lambda < 1$, so that the presence of the regulator reduces the production rate; for activating inputs, $\lambda > 1$. The $\lambda$ of inhibitory links is bound between 0.01 (strongest inhibition) and 1 (no inhibition) and that of the activatory links is bound between 100.0 (strongest activation) and 1.0 (no activation). 
%The shifted Hill functions are formulated such that the maximum production rate of any node is always $g_X$, making the maximum achievable expression $g_X/d_X$.  The fold-change parameter thus represents the regulatory efficacy of an input node, making it a natural target for studying structural stochasticity. 

We first simulated these networks deterministically using RACIPE~\cite{Huang2017}, a tool that allows us to explore the different configurations that the emergent phenotypic landscape can take across a large parameter space. RACIPE generates a large ensemble of ODE models by sampling multiple parameter sets (Figure~\ref{fig:1}B) and simulates each ODE model from multiple initial conditions until steady states are reached, resulting in an ensemble of steady states. We discretized expression of each node in each steady state into high (1) or low (0) based on the node's log-average expression across the ensemble of steady states (Figure~\ref{fig:1S1}B).  Threshold values for each network are given in Table~\ref{tb:Thresholds}. The models generated by RACIPE can be classified as monostable or bistable based on the number of discrete steady states they support, and further subdivided by the specific attractor configuration (see Table~\ref{tb:Attractors}). For the toggle switch, the dominant attractors were $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$, and the bistable $(0,1)\text{--}(1,0)$, as expected (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, i). Although a small fraction of models do permit $(1,1)$ as a steady state, the corresponding fold-change parameters are always close to 1, rendering inhibitions ineffective~\cite{hari_assessing_2023}. TSSA enhances bistability in the parameter space, with the other dominant attractors being $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$. Additionally, TSSA also supports tristability with $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ and either $(0, 0)$ or $(1, 1)$. The $(0, 0)$ state was accessed by a very small number of initial conditions, while $(1, 1)$ was more accessible (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, ii). TT and TTSA support single-high (one of the nodes has a high expression - $(0, 0, 1)$ etc.) and double-high (two of the nodes have high expression - $(0, 1, 1)$ etc.) nearly equally. TTSA enhances multistability over TT, promoting a tristable attractor with three single-high states (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, iii, iv).

We explored three noise modes (Section~\ref{sec:noise}) described by the equations below: 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Additive:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(t) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2) \\
\text{Multiplicative:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(t)\left(1 + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2)\right) \\
\text{Stationary:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(0) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2)
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Networks were simulated at eight levels of $\sigma$ 0.0, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5 and 1.0 scaled by the upper bound of $\lambda$ (1.0 for inhibitions and 100.0 for activations). The choices of $\sigma$ were made to ensure that low to moderate noise region has ample representation, as that region likely represents the underlying biology better than unrealistically high noises that span 50 or 100\% of the parameter range. The higher $\sigma$ values serve a more theoretical purpose in helping us understand the effect of stochastic simulations. The three noise modes produce qualitatively distinct trajectories of $\lambda$ over time . Stationary noise models normally distributed fluctuations around the initial value of $\lambda$ (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, i, \ref{fig:1S1}A, i). Additive noise models a constrained random walk of $\lambda$ bounded by the corresponding limits for activating and inhibiting $\lambda$ values. The resultant stationary distribution has a mean of 0.5 for inhibitory links and 50 for activatory links (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, ii, \ref{fig:1S1}A, ii). Multiplicative noise scales $\sigma$ by the current $\lambda$ value, such that lower noise values show lower deviation and higher noise values show higher deviation (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C, iii, \ref{fig:1S1}A, iii). The consequences of these distinct $\lambda$ trajectories for network state dynamics are explored in the following sections.


We sampled monostable and bistable models separately from RACIPE, weighting by the relative frequencies of each attractor configuration. We then performed stochastic simulations of these networks by periodically perturbing $\lambda$. For each parameter set, we generated 100 stochastic trajectories starting from randomly chosen initial conditions. We use the RACIPE ensemble-derived thresholds to discretize the stochastic trajectories as well (Figure~\ref{fig:1}D). Based on the number of discretized steady states allowed by a given parameter set in the deterministic simulations, each parameter set was characterized as monostable (single steady state), bistable, tristable and so on. For each network, we sampled 100 parameter sets per stability class and for each parameter set, we performed 100 stochastic simulations each to estimate the statistics. 



\subsection{High levels of stationary noise pushes the system towards hybrid states}

Stationary noise mimics the classical assumption underlying GRN models, that the fitted parameters are the mean of a distribution that does not significantly change the emergent phenotypic landscape from what the mean alone predicts. Thus, we started with analyzing the stochastic simulations under stationary noise to evaluate the validity of deterministic representation of regulatory parameters. We analyzed the stochastic trajectories by first discretizing them using the RACIPE ensemble threshold values (Figure~\ref{fig:2}A). The resultant discrete state trajectories are then used to calculate the residence time of each state, defined as the fraction of time steps a given trajectory sees that state. An average of the residence time over 100 trajectories, the mean residence time (MRT) is used to evaluate the occurrence of a state for a given parameter set under a given noise type and noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:2}B). Note that we use the same MRT calculation for deterministic simulations here on (e.g., $sigma = 0$). As deterministic simulations reach steady state by the burnin period (shaded in criss-crossed lines in Figure~\ref{fig:2}B), the MRT measures the relative size of the basin of attraction. For monostable parameters, MRT in deterministic simulations will always be 1, while multistable parameters will see fractional MRT corresponding to the basin size of the specific steady state in the attractor. 

As expected, the dominant states for a toggle switch remain as $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ for low to moderate $\sigma$ values under stationary noise. However, as the $\sigma$ increases to 0.5 and above (i.e., the noise scales as 50\% of the allowed range of $\lambda$), we see a drop in the MRT of the $(0,1)$ and (1, 0) states and a corresponding increase in the MRT of the (1, 1) state - which represents a co-expression of both nodes that inhibit each other (Figure~\ref{fig:2}C). Note that a similar increase is not seen in the MRT of the (0, 0) state (Figure~\ref{fig:2S1}A), an asymmetric outcome despite the symmetry of the stationary noise. We further studied TSSA, TT and TTSA under stationary noise and found them behaving similarly. TSSA, in absence of noise enables bistability in a larger fraction of parameter space than TS ($\approx 20\%$ in TS vs $>50\%$ in TSSA). TSSA also enables ~14\% of the state space to be tristable, with access to either $(0, 0)$ or the $(1, 1)$ state in addition to the dominant $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ states. However, the basin size of $(0, 0)$ state, is negligibly small, making $(0, 1) - (1, 0)-(1, 1)$ the only real tristable configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:2S1}B - tristable parameters at $\sigma = 0$). Similar to TS, TSSA maintains the characteristics of its phenotypic landscape under stationary noise - dominant $(0,1) and (1,0)$ states in monostable and bistable parameter sets, and dominant $(1, 1)$ state in tristable parameter sets for realistic values of noise, i.e., $\sigma \leq 0.1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:2}D). For higher $\sigma$, we see a dominance of $(1, 1)$ state. 

Toggle triad (TT) and toggle triad with self-activation (TTSA) dominantly enable two classes of steady states - single-high states ($(0, 0, 1); (0, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$ and double-high states ($(0, 1, 1); (1, 0, 1)$ and $(1, 1, 0)$). Previous studies have shown that toggle triad motif is more susceptible to various perturbations due to the inherent frustration in the network~\cite{harlapur2022functional, doi:10.1098/rsif.2020.0631}. Here, we see that the MRT distribution of TT starts to change for $\sigma > 0.01$, showing a higher sensitivity to noise than TS and TSSA motifs (Figure~\ref{fig:2}E, F, Figure~\ref{fig:2S1}C-D). At $0.01 \leq \sigma \leq 0.1$, the MRT of single-high states starts to go down and that of double-high states starts to come up, still qualitatively retaining the properties of the phenotypic landscape. However, $\sigma \geq 0.5$ leads to an increase in the MRT of the all-high state ($(1, 1, 1)$) in both TT and TTSA, similar to that of TS and TSSA, with no emergence of the all-low state ($(0, 0, 0)$). Overall, we find that the phenotypic landscape of both binary and tertiary cell-fate GRNs remains robust to stationary noise of low to moderate levels of $\sigma$. The response to larger noise can be better understood in our analysis of additive noise in the next section. 


\subsection{Additive noise reshapes the phenotypic landscape toward high-expression states, modulated by reachability and threshold margin}

Unlike stationary noise, additive noise starts to change the phenotypic landscape even at low $\sigma$ values. For toggle switch across a majority of the parameters, we see a decrease in the MRT of single-high states ($(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$) and an increase in the hybrid all-high ($(1, 1)$) state MRT (Figure~\ref{fig:3}A). The mean MRT of all-high state crosses 0.5 for $\sigma > 0.001$ (0.1\% of the lambda range) and stabilizes at 0.75 for $\sigma > 0.1$. Given the biological relevance of hybrid states showing co-expression of all nodes in developmental and pathological contexts, this shift in the phenotypic landscape of cell-fate decision motifs is a very significant result.

Interestingly, we note that while most parameters gravitate towards a high MRT for the all-high state for $\sigma \geq 0.01$, a smaller fraction of the parameters also show a significantly lower MRT of all-high state (Figure~\ref{fig:3}A). When we separately studied the parameter sets based on their multistability characteristics under deterministic conditions, we found that monostable states always had a lower MRT for all-high states under additive noise as compared to that of multistable parameter sets (Figure~\ref{fig:3}B). Given how readily bistable parameters converge to the all-high state, we hypothesized that the reduced MRT for monostable states could be because the all-high state is not accessible to some monostable parameter sets.  The upper limit of node expression in the ODE models we simulated is the ratio of the corresponding production and degradation rates. As we use a threshold derived from the ensemble steady state expression levels, it is possible that some parameter sets are sampled such that the upper limit of expression for one of the nodes defined by the production and degradation rates is lesser than the threshold. Since the bistable parameters constitute of two single-high states, bistable  parameter sets do not have this shortcoming. Thus, we wanted to understand whether it really is the case that monostable parameters that cannot access the all-high state are sampled, and in turn, if the access to all-high state is sufficient for the additive noise to push the system towards all-high state. 

Thus, we plotted the sampled parameter sets on the axes of "reachability" defined by the difference between the upper limit of a node's expression level and the threshold used the discretize that node. Reachability was satisfied across all sampled parameters — the production/degradation-derived expression ceiling exceeded threshold in every case. The observed variation in all-high MRT among monostable parameter sets therefore cannot be attributed to reachability itself (Figure~\ref{fig:3}C, \ref{fig:3S1}A). However, we see that the MRT of the all-high state increases with increasing distance between the maximum allowed value of the nodes and the corresponding thresholds. Across $\sigma$ values, the MRT of the all-high state correlated strongly positively with the smallest of the two difference values (Figure~\ref{fig:3}D). 

TSSA showed similar increase in hybrid (all-high) state MRT under additive noise. At low to moderate $\sigma$, TS and TSSA showed very similar MRT distributions. At high $\sigma$ values, TSSA monostable parameter sets showed a higher MRT of all-high states compared to TS (Figure~\ref{fig:3S1}B, i). This increase can be attributed to the inherent affinity TSSA has towards all-high state as discussed earlier. For TT and TTSA, we observed a transition of states from single-high to double-high to all-high, suggesting that while additive noise does push the system towards the all-high state, the transformation can be gradual as the network size increases (Figure~\ref{fig:3}E, \ref{fig:3S1}B, ii, iii). TT and TTSA have similar MRT distributions for low to moderate values of $\sigma$, with similar average MRT of all-high and double-high states. But at high values of $\sigma$, TTSA showed a higher MRT of the all-high state, with the double-high state average MRT approaching 0. TT on the other hand maintains a non-zero average MRT of double-high states even at higher $\sigma$ values (average MRT across all parameter sets - all-high 0.7 vs double-high 0.3 for TT vs all-high 0.9 and double-high 0.15 for TTSA). For all networks , we found a strong positive correlation between the difference quantity (max node expression minus threshold expression of the node) and the MRT of all-high states in monostable parameter sets. Mutlistable parameter sets showed relatively weaker correlations (Figure~\ref{fig:3}F). Thus, our analysis shows that additive noise increases the MRT of hybrid, all-high states in cell-fate decision networks. 

\subsection{Increased MRT of all-high states is caused by the $\lambda$ distribution and stochasticity}

Following the strong correlation we observed between parametrically determined upper bounds of node expression and the MRT of all-high state, we hypothesized that the drastic increase in the MRT of all-high states in comparison to deterministic simulations might also be explained by variation in the parameter space. Additive noise models a random walk in $\lambda$, bounded between 0.01 and 1 for inhibitory links and 1 and 100 for activating links. Any step crossing the boundary is absorbed at the boundary. For example, if $\lambda(t) + \eta(t) < 0.01$ then $\lambda(t+dt) = 0.01$. Due to the nature of random walk, the variance of lambda increases over time, unlike the stationary noise case. Furthermore, the absorbing boundaries increase the chances of $\lambda$ being at either of the borders than in the interior. The probability density of $\lambda$ at the borders increases with increasing $\sigma$, as at larger sigma values, there is a higher chance of taking larger $\lambda$ steps and correspondingly higher chances of hitting the boundaries (Figure ~\ref{fig:3b}A). Same boundary conditions are applied to other noise forms as well, and we see such boundary behavior in stationary noise at high $\sigma$, where $\lambda$ localizes at either of boundaries. While $\lambda$ closer to zero leads to a very strong inhibition by drastically reducing the net production rate, $\lambda$ closer to 1 leads to very weak inhibition and lets the production rate reach the highest values. Despite this symmetry in the emergent $\lambda$ distribution, the shift in the landscape is asymmetric towards all-high states. One potential reason could be the way $\lambda$ parameter is sampled in RACIPE for inhibitory interactions. The fold-change parameter is first sampled between 1 and 100 and then the inverse of that number is used for inhibitory links. Thus the distribution of $\lambda$ for inhibitory links is a log-uniform distribution tailed heavily towards lower values of $\lambda$ with a mean of $\approx0.05$. As the stationary distribution of $\lambda$ in additive noise has a mean of 0.5, inhibitory links experience a significant increase in the average $\lambda$, which in turn leads to weakening of the inhibition. Thus we wanted to know if this shift in $\lambda$ distribution is enough to explain the increase in the MRT of all-high states. For each parameter set used for stochastic simulations, we sampled $\lambda$ from the additive noise stationary distribution shown in (Figure ~\ref{fig:3b}A). For each such $\lambda$ combination, we simulated the networks deterministically ($\sigma = 0$) at multiple randomly sampled initial conditions and calculate the mean frequency of each discrete state across initial conditions, which is further averaged over different lambda values. While the original RACIPE parameters for TS and TT have a very small fraction of parameter sets with non-zero frequency of all-high states, majority of resampled simulations led to a non-zero frequency of all-high states, reaching up to 0.9 in TS and up to 0.75 in TT (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}B, \ref{fig:3bS1}A). TSSA and TTSA, which inherently stabilize the all-high states due to the self-activation links, resampled simulations still led to an increase in the frequency of all-high states for a majority of the parameter sets (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}B, \ref{fig:3bS1}A). Thus, the $\lambda$ distribution does contribute to increasing the MRT of all-high state. Interestingly, however, when we compared the MRT of all-high states in stochastic simulations and the resampled deterministic simulations, we found that the stochastic MRT was higher across a large fraction of parameters for all four networks (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}C, i, , \ref{fig:3bS1}B). Furthermore, a sigmoidal relationship emerges between the stochastic MRT and the deterministic resampled frequency of all-high state (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}C, ii, \ref{fig:3bS1}C). Below a threshold value of all-high state frequency under deterministic simulations with resampled $\lambda$, stochastic MRT is lower than or equal to deterministic frequency, and vice-versa (Figure ~\ref{fig:3b}C). The specific threshold MRT is network and $\sigma$ dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}C, ii, \ref{fig:3bS1}C). Across all four networks and $\sigma$ values, the MRT of all-high states increased significantly from original to resampled deterministic to stochastic simulations (Figure ~\ref{fig:3b}D). We further tested the effect of stochasticity by reducing the frequency of update of $\lambda$, i.e., the $dt$ value in Equation~\ref{eq:lambda}, from 0.01 to 1.0 and 10.0 (Figure~\ref{fig:3bS3}). As the frequency of update decreased, the system had more time to relax towards a steady state, thereby losing the effects of stochasticity. Correspondingly, we see a reduction in the MRT of all-high state and increase in the MRT of the native states of these networks at lower $\sigma$ values. At higher sigma values, the noise compensates for the relaxation, pushing the systems back to hybrid states. These results indicate that, while the distribution of $\lambda$ does shift the landscape towards the all-high hybrid state, the stochastic simulations serve to further enhance this shift.

To isolate the mechanism by which regulatory ($\lambda$) noise redistributes occupancy toward high-expression states, we analyzed the toggle switch in the Boolean limit of node expression and $\lambda$ distribution, where the exact stationary distribution can be computed in closed form for both the deterministic (fixed-$\lambda$) and stochastic (fluctuating-$\lambda$) cases (see Supplementary section \ref{sec:boolean}). We noticed a crucial asymmetry in the stochastic simulations that is absent in deterministic simulations. For an inhibitory edge, the shifted Hill function entering the production term is
\begin{equation}
  H^{S}(x,\lambda) \;=\; H^{-}(x) \;+\; \lambda\,H^{+}(x),
  \qquad H^{+}(x) \equiv 1 - H^{-}(x),
  \label{eq:hshift}
\end{equation}

Under the extreme case of $\sigma=1$, where the $\lambda$ walk is confined to the two boundaries of its range, $\lambda\in\{\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}\}$ with $\lambda_{\min}\approx 0.01$ and $\lambda_{\max}=1$. Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:hshift} at these two limits exposes a structural asymmetry:
\begin{align}
  \lambda = 1: \quad & H^{S}(x,1) = H^{-}(x)+H^{+}(x) = 1
    && \text{(independent of } x\text{)},\\
  \lambda \approx 0.01: \quad & H^{S}(x,0.01) \approx H^{-}(x)
    && \text{(depends on } x\text{)}.
\end{align}
The $\lambda=1$ boundary abolishes regulation \emph{unconditionally}: the target node is produced at its full rate regardless of the regulator's expression. The $\lambda\approx 0$ boundary imposes repression only \emph{conditionally}: it lowers the target's production rate only when the regulator $x$ is high, and has essentially no effect when the regulator is low ($H^{-}(x)\!\approx\!1$). An excursion toward de-repression ($\lambda=1$) therefore always ``fires,'' whereas an excursion toward repression fires only when the partner node happens to be expressed. This unconditional-up / conditional-down asymmetry is the microscopic origin of the enhancement. We see this clearly in the boolean approximation of node expression (i.e., each node can only take two expression levels - either 1 or 0). In the extreme $\sigma$ condition, resampled deterministic simulations can only converge to the all-high state if they start from the all-high state (a frequency of 0.25). However, in stochastic simulations, asymmetry in the response of shifted hill function ensures that a strongly repressed state is maintained only when the source node is on, or when both are off. This asymmetry leads to a higher probability (equivalent to MRT) of all-high state in the stationary distribution of the stochastic process. Even at lower values of $\sigma$, this asymmetry holds true by making the de-repressed state more easily accessible to the nodes of the network than the repressed states. Therefore, we conclude that the drift in the phenotypic landscape caused towards the all-high state caused by additive noise is a combined outcome of the shift in the parameter space and the asymmetry exploited by stochastic simulations. 


\subsection{Multiplicative noise drives the phenotypic landscape toward single-high states - an opposite effect as that of additive noise}

We then analyzed the effect of multiplicative noise in $\lambda$ on the phenotypic landscape. Briefly, multiplicative noise models a random walk in $\lambda$ with a state-dependent step size. For inhibitory interactions, the noise amplitude is smaller near $\lambda \approx 0$ and larger near $\lambda \approx 1$. This asymmetry makes $\lambda$ approach over $0$ over time, unlike additive noise where $\lambda$ visits both the boundaries and the intermediate values at intermediate $\sigma$ (Figure~\ref{fig:1}D). While this concentration of $\lambda$ near $0$ may seem like a deterministic behavior, stochasticity has a strong impact on the outcome of multiplicative noise, even more so than additive noise in certain cases as we will see below. 

Intuitively, multiplicative noise enhances repression in the edges of toggle switch, thereby driving the system strongly towards single-high states (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}A, i). This does not affect the phenotypic landscape of TS, as single-high states were dominant to begin with (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C). TSSA also showed a similar gravitation towards single-high states despite TSSA also stabilizing the all-high state in absence of noise (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}A, ii). The enhancement of single-high state by multiplicative noise is prominently seen in case of TT and TTSA. Both these networks have a significantly high frequency of double-high state along with single-high state in absence of noise (Figure~\ref{fig:1}C). In presence of multiplicative noise, both these networks converge to single-high states, achieving a high MRT (~1) by $\sigma = 0.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}A, iii,~\ref{fig:4}A). Thus, consistently across the four networks studied so far, we found that multiplicative noise promotes the frequency of single-high states (Figure~\ref{fig:4}B). At very high $\sigma$ values, TTSA shows a decrease in the frequency of single-high states with a corresponding increase in double-high state MRT. At high $\sigma$, $\lambda$ starts to approach higher values purely based on the fluctuations (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}B). However, the probability distribution of $\lambda$ still remains concentrated dominantly near the lower limit, resulting in an increase of double-high states only and not all-high states. To further validate this result, we simulated three new networks that are seen in cell-fate decision systems. The first network is an extension of TT network in the context of T cell differentiation, including the regulation of T-reg cells through the transcription factor Foxp3. The resultant four node network is referred to as toggle tetrahedron (TT4), and is composed of four nodes mutually inhibiting each other. Previous works have explored this network and its self-activation counterpart (TT4SA), our second 4 node network, as candidates for four-fate differentiation~\cite{Duddu2024MultistabilityDifferentiation}  (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}C, i, ii). However, this and other candidate networks for multi-fate systems have so far failed to support the emergence of single-high states representative of fully differentiated cell-fates. TT4 and TT4SA, specifically support only double-high steady states (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}D). Given the properties of multiplicative noise seen so far, we wanted to study if the double-high states of TT4 and TT4SA could also be driven towards single-high states. The third network is a combination of two mutually inhibiting toggle switches that we refer to as TS4, a representation of coupled cell-fate decision systems often seen in biology~\cite{pasani2020hybrid, hari2022emergent}. TS4 also supports double-high states (Figure~\ref{fig:4S1}D). However, unlike TT4 where all six possible double-high states have equal frequency, TS4 only supports a limited number of double-high states that represent the coexistence of the two binary cell-fate systems. Unlike sequential differentiation, coupled systems are expected to have co-existing phenotypes governed by the network structure. Thus, we asked whether multiplicative noise pushes these coexisting double-high states into single-high states, irrespective of the topology. Under additive noise, all three networks lead to a consistent increase in all-high state, despite the differences in the network structure (Figure~\ref{fig:4S2}A). Under multiplicative noise, TT4 saw an increase in the single-high state MRT with $\sigma$, followed by a decrease brought on due to the boundary sampling of $\lambda$ (Figure~\ref{fig:4}C, \ref{fig:4S2}B). However, TT4SA maintained a double-high dominant landscape (Figure~\ref{fig:4S2}B). TT4SA did show a decrease in double-high state MRT as multiplicative noise increased, with a corresponding increase in the MRT of single-high states. However, this change never put single-high states in a dominant position. Furthermore, TS4 also maintained a double-high dominant landscape, with no change in the MRT with $\sigma$, preserving the behavior of internal toggle switches. Thus, unlike additive noise, the effect of multiplicative noise on the phenotypic landscape is dependent on the network topology. 

Given the potentially deterministic nature of multiplicative noise, we tried to dissociate the effects of the $\lambda$ distribution from that of stochasticity in these simulations. Thus, we generated resampled deterministic simulations similar to that of Figure~\ref{fig:3b}C. For TS and TSSA, the difference between stochastic and resampled deterministic MRT was very low, as expected (Figure~\ref{fig:4}D). However, TT, TTSA and TT4 showed a high difference even at lower values of $\sigma$ (< $0.05$, Figure~\ref{fig:4}D). In comparison, additive noise simulations required higher  $\sigma$ ($\geq 0.1$) to achieve this level of difference (Figure~\ref{fig:3b}C). TT4SA and TS4 also showed minimal difference, in accordance with their MRT trends. These differences were mirrored by corresponding decreases in the MRTs of single-high states (Figure~\ref{fig:4S2}D, E). Interestingly, however, the MRTs of original deterministic simulations and resampled simulations were nearly identical (Figure~\ref{fig:4S2}F, G), indicating that this "fixation" towards terminal differentiation seen in multiplicative noise is entirely due to the stochasticity, unlike the additive noise where the $\lambda$ distributions also contributed to the increase in MRT of all-high states. We further tested this behavior by simulating these networks at larger dt values (Figure~\ref{fig:5S3}). Unlike additive noise, increasing dtdt dt did not reduce the excess of stochastic MRT over the resampled-deterministic MRT for multiplicative noise. Since larger dtdt dt gives node expression more time to relax toward the steady state set by the current $\lambda$ before the next update, this indicates that multiplicative noise's dynamical contribution — unlike additive noise's — is not primarily a consequence of expression lagging behind a fast-moving $\lambda$. The specific mechanism by which individual stochastic realizations of $\lambda$ produce this persistent excess remains to be characterized, but our results indicate it is distinct from the timescale-separation (non-adiabatic) effect identified for additive noise.

% We wanted to further understand the nature of these transitions - which single-high states do the hybrid double-high states converge to? Can multiple single-high states be achieved from a given double-high state - i.e., if we think of the double-high state as a progenitor, can it always differentiate into multiple terminal phenotypes? We chose to do this analysis on TT4, as this network has the highest frequency of double-high and lowest frequency of single-high states, thereby giving us the cleanest signal with a large dataset. TT4 predominantly allows monostable and bistable double-high steady states. We first analyzed the monostable steady states, and found that the single-high states emergent from the application of multiplicative noise were always the closest single-high states from the double-high states, achieved by pushing the expression of one of the high expression nodes to low. Bistable states of TT4 are composed of "mirror" states, i.e., different pairs of nodes have high expression in both the states. Therefore, the bistable double-high states have access to all four of the single-high states. We further find that while all these parmeters converge to single-high states upon application of appropriate multiplicative noise, a large fraction of parameters a small fraction of parameter sets (~19\% for TT, 33\% for TT4) converge to two single-high states in different instances of simulations. We further studied the transitions observed in multiplicative noise simulations and found that the only transitions are between the double-high states and single-high states, and never between the two single-high states. Furthermore the number of transitions observed are also very small (1 in every x time steps). Since we know that the initial time steps are the most noisy in multiplicative noise, most of these transitions would have happened initially, resulting in stable terminal states achieved after long-term application of multiplicative noise. We were able to confirm these results for TT, TTSA and TT4SA. Therefore, we concluded that multiplicative noise can push the landscape of cell-fate decision systems towards single-high, terminally differentiated states. 


\subsection{Effects of regulatory noise on the phenotypic landscape scale to larger biological networks}

While the toggle switch and TSSA are well-studied representatives of binary cell-fate decision systems, the regulatory circuits underlying real cell-fate transitions are rarely as small or as isolated. Phenotypes emerge from coordinated expression across tens to hundreds of genes, many of which function as transcription factors that individually regulate transitions between phenotypes. It is therefore important to ask whether the noise-driven dynamics observed in small motifs generalise to larger, more complex GRNs. To address this, we asked whether biological GRNs governing two well-studied cell-fate decisions -- Epithelial mesenchymal plasticity~\cite{hari2022landscape} and Gonadal cell-fate decision~\cite{rios2015boolean} -- behave consistently.

Large-scale GRNs underlying binary cell-fate decisions share a common coarse-grained structure: nodes can be partitioned into two ``teams'', with intra-team interactions predominantly activating and inter-team interactions predominantly inhibitory (Figure~\ref{fig:5S1}). At a coarse-grained level, this structure is analogous to a TSSA, with each super-node representing the collective of genes defining one phenotype. The biological GRNs adhere to this structure at a coarse-grained level as well. The EMT network, a 23-node network underlying epithelial--mesenchymal transition (EMT)~\cite{Huang2017}, has two teams of nodes corresponding to the epithelial and mesenchymal phenotypes respectively. In fact, we previously showed that all large-scale EMT GRNs constructed previously could be coarse-grained in two mutually inhibiting teams, and that the presence of these teams gives rise to a robust, low-dimensional phenotypic landscape despite the size of these networks~\cite{hari2022landscape, Hari2025LowNetworks}. We also showed that these teamed networks are robust to noise in the network state, and extensive mutations disrupting the network of positive feedback present in these GRNs are required to stabilize what is otherwise weakly stable hybrid phenotype~\cite{rashid2022network, Hari2025APlasticity}. The 19-node gonadal cell-fate decision network cell-fate decisions between Sertoli and Granulosa fates that are upstream of male and female development respectively~\cite{rios2015boolean} has two teams of nodes corresponding to Sertoli and Granulosa phenotypes, and carry the advantages of team structure across simulation formalisms~\cite{Hari2025LowNetworks, Shyam2025MutuallyNetworks}.

Given the size of these networks, exploration of regulatory noise using similar ODE based models as with small-scale networks earlier would be computationally infeasible. Hence, we utilized a threshold based boolean formulation to analyze these networks instead \cite{FontClos2018}. In this formalism, each node is allowed to take either -1 (low) or +1 (high) expression levels. The edges of the network are given a weight of +1 (activation) or -1 (inhibition). The state of the network is updated asynchronously, based on an ising-model inspired, additive rule (see Methods). The binary state vectors ($S \in \{0, 1\}^N$) were converted into team scores --- the fraction of nodes in each team that are active --- and a phenotypic score defined as the difference between the two team scores. Values near $\pm 1$ indicate terminal phenotypes (epithelial/mesenchymal or Sertoli/Granulosa), while values near 0 indicate hybrid states. Under deterministic conditions, the phenotypic landscape is largely concentrated in regions corresponding to terminal states with team scores $\pm$ 1.

Under additive noise, both biological networks show behaviour consistent with the small network analysis. Trajectories initiated from terminal phenotypes of the unperturbed network rapidly migrate to hybrid phenotypic scores and remain there throughout the simulation, regardless of initial condition (Figure~\ref{fig:5}A). The transition occurs sharply: even at $\sigma = 0.001$, terminal state frequencies are markedly reduced (Figure~\ref{fig:5}B), and by $\sigma = 0.005$ the phenotypic score distribution has saturated into a stable hybrid-dominated configuration that changes little with further increases in noise (Figure~\ref{fig:5}C, \ref{fig:5S2}A). Multiplicative and stationary noise produce minimal disruption to the phenotypic landscape, with trajectories retaining high terminal state frequency similar to the unperturbed network across all noise levels tested (Figure~~\ref{fig:5}C, \ref{fig:5S2}B, C).

The two networks differ, however, in the characteristics of their hybrid states. In the EMT network, the hybrid phenotypic score distribution retains some polarisation toward $\pm 0.5$--$0.75$, reflecting a tendency for one team to remain more active than the other even in the hybrid regime (Figure~\ref{fig:5}C(i), B). In the Gonadal network, the distribution collapses tightly to near zero, indicating near-equal team expression with no residual polarization (Figure~\ref{fig:5}C(ii)). Both networks have similar densities and team structures to each other,  suggesting that network size and specific wiring topology beyond the team structure are likely contributors to this difference. A full characterization of the structural features that determine the degree of polarization within the hybrid regime remains an open question. Overall, our results indicate that regulatory noise can have a significant impact on the phenotypic landscape, with the direction of the impact determined by the nature and the magnitude of the noise. While additive noise can drive the cell-fate decision system up towards an undifferentiated phenotype, multiplicative noise can drive the system down towards a terminally differentiated state (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}).

\section{Discussion}

Regulatory interactions in gene networks are rarely static. Transcription factor binding affinities fluctuate due to conformational heterogeneity, post-translational modification, and competition for genomic binding sites, yet GRN models overwhelmingly treat these interaction strengths as fixed parameters. Static models have nonetheless been remarkably successful at predicting cell-fate outcomes, from EMT circuits to Boolean perturbation frameworks. This success is not a reason to dismiss stochasticity in kinetic parameters. Instead, we must ask what such noise would be mistaken for if it were present. Here, we studied the consequences of allowing the fold-change parameter which sets the regulatory efficacy of each interaction to vary stochastically over time, across cell-fate decision motifs of increasing complexity and two well-characterised biological GRNs (EMT and gonadal cell-fate determination).

\paragraph{Regulatory noise can cloud the inference of network topology}
Prior theoretical work has shown that noise can shift or stretch the effective bistable range of a network relative to its deterministic prediction, and can generate bimodal, switch-like population distributions in circuits that are deterministically monostable for all parameter values~\cite{OchabMarcinek2010w}. Regulatory noise of the kind we studied here also modifies the qualitative details of the phenotypic landscape. The (1,1) co-expression state, which is absent or negligible in the steady state distribution emergent from toggle switch in the deterministic limit, becomes dominant under additive noise, with mean residence time (MRT) rising to $\sim$0.6 for monostable parameter sets and $\sim$0.85--0.9 for bistable ones by $\sigma = 0.1$. This suggests that a positive correlation between two genes observed in a population snapshot like transcriptomic data would not be able to distinguish between mutual activation under deterministic conditions and mutual inhibition under stochastic conditions. Similarly, the single-high states that are not stable in larger networks such as the toggle tetrahedron become dominant under multiplicative noise. Previous simulations have shown that co-existence of single-high states beyond three node networks is not feasible. A network's population-level phenotype distribution can therefore reflect its noise regime as much as its wiring diagram. Inferring topology from steady-state expression data without accounting for regulatory noise therefore risks systematically over- or under-estimating regulatory complexity --- precisely the motivation for modelling regulatory noise explicitly rather than treating it as a second-order correction.

\paragraph{The three noise modes map onto different modes of action of IDP transcription factors}
The three noise modes studied here - stationary, additive and multiplicative each corresponds to a mechanistically distinct route by which an intrinsically disordered transcription factor's conformational ensemble can modulate its effective regulatory strength. Each noise mode redistributes state occupancy in a qualitatively different direction.

Stationary noise, where $\lambda$ fluctuates around an initially sampled $\lambda$ value, has the smallest effect of the three modes at low-to-moderate $\sigma$. But it is not completely inert: at the highest noise levels tested, it produces a modest rise in hybrid-state MRT that is directionally consistent with, though substantially weaker than, the additive noise effect. This is the same boundary-driven mechanism as additive noise, operating from a much higher noise threshold rather than being a qualitatively distinct, noise-immune regime. Molecularly, this mode corresponds to the inherent disorderedness present in TFs, which does not affect the TF's binding to the promoter, but is theorized to help flexibly search for and recruit the transcriptional machinery~\cite{Holehouse2023TheRegions, Jonas2025IntrinsicallySearch}. The mean $\lambda$ that this distribution reverts to represents a deep valley in the TF-promoter-transcription machinery ensemble, with the noise representing minor fluctuations around the valley that can be captured by a deterministic description of the system.  

Multiplicative noise, where perturbations scale with the current value of $\lambda$ such that inhibitory $\lambda$ is driven toward zero, single-high states are reinforced or actively restored (even the rare deterministic TS models that permit (1,1) show that state suppressed), and in TT, double-high hybrid states present in the deterministic attractor are converted to single-high states. Molecularly, this models a higher disorderedness in TFs than represented by Stationary noise, and captures the conformational sampling that progressively locks the ensemble toward one sub-state. While this behavior is not well studied in the context of TFs, specific post-translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation, stabilise particular IDP conformers with half-lives of minutes to hours~\cite{Kulkarni2022, Kulkarni2019}. Because each such modification event can make subsequent modification more likely, the process compounds in a state-dependent, self-reinforcing manner --- the same qualitative signature multiplicative noise produces on $\lambda$. 

Additive noise, in which $\lambda$ performs a bounded random walk, evolving into a distribution independent of its initial value. Additive noise progressively weakens inhibitory interactions and destabilises single-high attractors in favour of hybrid co-expression states, an effect that intensifies with network complexity, with the MRT of all-high states reaching ~1.0 at high $\sigma$ as long as the state is accessible by the parameter set. Molecularly, this maps most directly onto unconstrained conformational diffusion agnostic to any contact with the DNA: when an IDP's disordered region samples its ensemble without being biased toward any particular sub-state, its influence on effective fold-change varies in a manner unanchored to the interaction's current value --- precisely the additive case~\cite{Ferrie2022Structure-functionRegulation, Holehouse2023TheRegions}. 


Together, these three molecular readings recover the diversity of IDP-mediated fold-change fluctuation --- unbiased, locking, and state-anchored --- that motivated our use of multiple noise modes rather than a single generic noise term.


\paragraph{The stochastic-vs-deterministic comparison indicates a genuine dynamical contribution, not merely a redistribution of parameters.}
A natural objection to the additive and multiplicative noise results above is that they might simply reflect resampling: if additive noise broadens or shifts the effective distribution of $\lambda$ values explored, then running deterministic RACIPE on parameter sets drawn from that same noise-induced $\lambda$ distribution might reproduce the same state occupancies without requiring any dynamical argument. We tested this directly by comparing the MRT obtained from actual stochastic trajectories against the MRT obtained from deterministic simulations sampled from the stationary $\lambda$-distribution that each noise mode generates. 
%For multiplicative noise, and for fluctuating noise at low-to-moderate $\sigma$, the two approaches agree closely, indicating that in these regimes the system is close to adiabatic: expression tracks its instantaneous equilibrium at each sampled $\lambda$, and the outcome is fully explained by where $\lambda$ spends its time. 
For both additive and multiplicative noise, the stochastic MRT of high-expression (hybrid) states and single-high (terminally differentiated) states respectively, exceeds what the $\lambda$-distribution alone predicts. This indicates that additive and multiplicative noise contribute a dynamical effect beyond parameter redistribution --- consistent with the system being pushed away from the adiabatic limit, so that the temporal structure of the $\lambda$ trajectory itself, not merely its stationary distribution, shapes which states are visited. This distinction matters for interpretation: it means additive and multiplicative regulatory noise are not simply equivalent to sampling from a noisier RACIPE parameter ensemble, and that non-adiabatic coupling between conformational and expression timescales is itself a biologically relevant variable.

\paragraph{The effect generalises beyond small motifs to two biological GRNs.}
To test whether these motif-level results are a generic property of larger biological networks rather than an artefact of the toggle switch or triad specifically, we simulated two real biological GRNs: a 23-node EMT network and a 19-node gonadal cell-fate network (Sertoli/Granulosa determination), using a Boolean formalism, under the same three noise modes. Both showed the same qualitative behaviour as the small network motifs, and the transition was sharp. Under additive noise, terminal-state frequency was already markedly reduced at $\sigma = 0.001$ and the phenotypic score distribution had saturated into a hybrid-dominated configuration by $\sigma = 0.005$, essentially independent of initial condition. Multiplicative and stationary noise again produced minimal disruption. The two networks differed, however, in the character of their hybrid states: the EMT network retained partial polarisation ($\pm 0.5$--0.75) even in the hybrid regime, indicating one team tends to remain more active than the other, whereas the gonadal network collapsed fully to the (0.5, 0.5) team-score centre. Both networks have comparable density and team strucutres, indicating that this difference must be the property of other network properties, such as network size or specific wiring beyond team structure, that we have not yet characterized (see Limitations).

\paragraph{Hybrid states, entropy, and pathological implications.}
A unifying consequence of additive regulatory noise, across every topology and scale studied here, is an increase in phenotypic entropy: the system spends progressively more time in co-expression states that are absent from, or negligible in, the deterministic attractor landscape. In the EMT network specifically, these co-expression states correspond directly to the hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal (hybrid E/M) phenotype. The hybrid E/M state is now well-established as a functionally distinct and clinically relevant cell state: hybrid E/M cells display elevated stemness, enhanced collective migration, greater metastatic competence, and stronger therapy resistance relative to cells occupying either terminal phenotype~\cite{Pastushenko2019EMTMetastasis, jolly2015implications, pasani2020hybrid, Sahoo2021}. Our results suggest a concrete biophysical route through which hybrid E/M incidence could be elevated in tumours: if regulatory noise in regulatory interactions is increased --- by the tumour microenvironment, or by non-genetic or genetic alterations to TF binding --- additive-like fold-change fluctuations are, on their own, sufficient to drive both an isolated bistable motif and a full 23-node biological network into a predominantly hybrid regime, with a transition threshold ($\sigma \sim 0.001$--0.005) well within a biologically plausible range. This directly links molecular-scale disorder in transcriptional regulation to population-level phenotypic heterogeneity of the kind that fuels tumour progression and drug tolerance.

\paragraph{Stem cell identity and chromatin state.}
There is a strong correspondence between our model and developmental biology that extends to pluripotency. We build on the established view that hybrid, co-expressing states reflect a less differentiated, higher-entropy configuration, while single-high terminal states reflect the low-dimensional, more homogeneous endpoint of differentiation~\cite{Teschendorff2017Single-cellTranscriptome, Hari2025LowNetworks}. Pluripotent stem cells maintain a globally open, decondensed chromatin state supporting stochastic, broadly accessible transcription~\cite{Meshorer2006HyperdynamicCells, Efroni2008GlobalCells}, consistent with recent evidence that fluctuations in chromatin accessibility are actively amplified to permit ectopic, multi-lineage-like gene expression programs in a physiological setting~\cite{Gamble2025}. In our framework, this corresponds to an additive-noise regime: open chromatin raises the density of accessible genomic sites, increasing the likelihood that a TF engages non-specific sites and modulates its effective regulatory strength in a manner unanchored to its current interaction state.

Our results yield a distinct, testable prediction from this correspondence: transition towards a terminally differentiated state does not require a specific instructive signal to execute the transition itself. Instead, we propose a two-component picture : extrinsic signals bias which hybrid or lineage-primed state a progenitor occupies, while a shift toward a multiplicative-noise-like regime supplies the drift that resolves that hybrid state into a single-high terminal fate, without requiring the signal to specify the differentiation step itself. This separates two components of differentiation that are typically treated as a single instructive event: fate selection (signal-dependent) and fate resolution (noise-mode-dependent, signal-agnostic). This view helps reconcile the longstanding debate over whether lineage commitment is fundamentally instructive or noise-driven~\cite{<lineage commitment uncertainty refs>}, and offers a mechanistic account of how both could be simultaneously true at different stages of the same transition.

\paragraph{IDPs, conformational noise, and cancer.}
Cancer mutations targeting IDPs are of particular relevance given the mapping above. Mutations within disordered regions can increase non-specific binding affinity by expanding the conformationally accessible ensemble, and can introduce new disordered segments into previously structured TFs, collectively amplifying the magnitude of additive-like fold-change fluctuations~\cite{Kulkarni2023IntrinsicallyLater}. Under our framework, such mutations are predicted to increase hybrid-phenotype frequency and phenotypic heterogeneity within a tumour even without any change to deterministic network topology --- a functional interpretation of IDR mutations beyond direct loss-of-function, and a motivation for quantifying the conformational-noise contribution of specific cancer-associated IDR variants.

\paragraph{Decoupling conformational drift from binding: justifying the bounds.}
Our model decouples the noise in regulatory strength from the binding event itself. From the IDP perspective, conformational drift need not be gated by whether the transcription factor is currently bound; the disordered region can sample its accessible ensemble whether or not it is engaged at a promoter. What is bounded is not the conformational search but the downstream consequence of any given conformation: there is an upper limit on how efficiently any TF--DNA interaction can drive transcription, set by factors external to the TF's own conformational landscape, such as polymerase recruitment kinetics and the physical throughput of the transcription machinery, and symmetrically a floor, since a fold-change cannot fall below the point of no functional engagement. These bounds are therefore a direct outcome of treating conformational sampling and transcriptional output as separate processes linked only through a saturating map, not a numerical convenience. This is also why we implement the bounds as hard limits rather than a soft, binding-aware boundary: a boundary that softens as it is approached would require the underlying mechanism to sense the binding event it is nominally decoupled from, which we consider a less general assumption than a hard ceiling on function that is agnostic to how it is reached.

\paragraph{The sign-change assumption.}
A consequence of this implementation is that inhibitory fold-changes ($\lambda \in [\lambda_{min},1]$) and activatory fold-changes ($\lambda \in [1, \lambda_{max}]$) occupy disjoint intervals under every noise mode studied here: no amount of additive, multiplicative, or stationary fluctuation can carry an interaction across $\lambda$=1 to invert its sign. Continuous modulation of an existing interaction's strength (this study) and discrete topological rewiring, including sign inversion (deferred), are therefore disjoint mechanisms by construction, not merely different in degree. A molecular route to genuine sign inversion is nonetheless well documented: transcription factors such as RBPJ switch between repressive and activating output depending on which corepressor or coactivator complex they currently engage~\cite{Dong2026MolecularFunctions}, a behaviour formalised as "incoherent" regulation, in which a single factor acts as both activator and repressor at a given target under a fixed molecular context~\cite{Martinez-Corral2025EmergenceContext}. Because our additive-noise results show hybrid states increasing in frequency without any edge changing sign, we read this as continuous noise \emph{phenocopying} part of the phenotypic signature normally attributed to rewiring, not as evidence that rewiring has occurred. Extending the present formalism to allow genuine sign inversion, and quantifying how much of the rewiring phenotype can be reproduced by noise alone, is left to future work.

\paragraph{Limitations and future directions.}
Several aspects of this study warrant further investigation. First, the fold-change noise model is, by design, abstract in its mechanistic scope: it captures the effect of molecular fluctuation on regulatory strength without specifying its precise source, which limits direct quantitative comparison with specific experimental measurements of TF binding dynamics; mapping our noise modes onto measurable quantities such as single-molecule TF residence times or IDR conformational correlation times would strengthen this connection. Second, our formalism perturbs fold-change parameters, which is distinct from classical state-space (expression-level) noise of the Langevin type; because such noise acts within a fixed landscape and is continuously damped back toward the current attractor. We expect it to have a comparatively limited effect relative to fold-change noise, which can deform the landscape itself. Studies of noise-driven differentiation predict a rescaling of the existing attractors in the landscape~\cite{Herrera2024ADifferentiation, Mojtahedi2016CellTransition}. We had previously shown that combining state-space noise with edge perturbations can introduce new dynamical behavior in the same attractor landscape \cite{Hari2025APlasticity}, which is closer to our predictions here, but fundamentally different still, as the fold-change perturbations generate new attractors in the landscape (hybrid states in additive noise and terminal states in TT4 in multiplicative noise). A formal, sensitivity-matched comparison between the two noise classes remains future work. Third, the treatment of activatory interactions under multiplicative noise requires further development, since the asymmetry between inhibitory and activatory parameters under state-dependent noise introduces complexities the current formalism does not fully address. Fourth, for the large biological networks our analysis is limited to the Boolean framework, which sacrifices quantitative resolution of expression levels; an ODE-based treatment under parameter noise, while computationally demanding, would allow more direct comparison with the small-motif results and with single-cell expression data. Fifth, the difference in hybrid-state polarization between the EMT and gonadal networks under additive noise remains mechanistically unexplained and points to structural features beyond density and impurity that we have not yet characterised. Sixth, the current formalism applies noise uniformly across all regulatory edges; because the aberrant conformational dynamics associated with IDP mutation are, mechanistically, localised events, only a subset of interactions in a large network may be affected at any given time. Empirical estimates of interactor turnover under a single phosphorylation-state transition in PAGE4/MED25-driven regulation suggest that roughly a quarter of stable interactions can be affected by a single such event, which we take as a rough guide to the scale of localisation worth exploring in future work, ideally validated on small networks before being extended to the large biological networks studied here. Finally, our present results establish the direction and generality of each noise mode's effect but do not yet resolve how noise mode interacts with multistability itself --- for instance, whether multiplicative noise can convert monostable parameter regimes into multistable ones by resolving symmetric attractors stochastically, or whether this generalises across the full network set we have simulated; these results require further verification and are not reported here.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Network models and RACIPE ensemble generation}\label{sec:RACIPE}

Each network motif is modelled as a system of ordinary differential equations (ODE), one ODE per node, following the shifted Hill formalism of RACIPE~\cite{Huang2017}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dX}{dt} = g_X \prod_{i \in P} \frac{H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i})}{\lambda_{Y_i}} \prod_{i \in N} H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i}) - d_X X
\label{eq:odeM}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H(Y_i, Y_{i0}, n_{Y_i}, \lambda_{Y_i}) = \lambda_{Y_i} \frac{Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}}{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}} + Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}} + \frac{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}}}{Y_{i0}^{\,n_{Y_i}} + Y_i^{\,n_{Y_i}}}
\label{eq:hillM}
\end{equation}
where $g_X$ and $d_X$ are the production and degradation rates of node $X$, $P$ and $N$ index its activating and inhibiting regulators, and $\lambda_{Y_i}$ is the fold-change by which regulator $Y_i$ modulates $X$'s production ($\lambda_{Y_i} > 1$ for activation, $\lambda_{Y_i} < 1$ for inhibition).

For each network topology, RACIPE~\cite{Huang2017} was used to generate an ensemble of $10{,}000$ parameter sets by uniform sampling within standard biologically plausible ranges, with each parameter set solved from 100 random initial conditions to identify its steady-state attractor(s). The resultant ensemble of steady states was used to calculate thresholds of individual node expressions ($\theta_X$) as the average expression across the ensemble, which were then used to discretize the steady states into strings of 1's and 0's. Based on the number of such discretized steady states allowed, each parameter set was assigned a multistability class (monostable, bistable, tristable, tetrastable). A representative subset of 100 parameter sets was selected from each stability class present for a given network for downstream stochastic analysis. 

\subsection{Regulatory noise model} \label{sec:noise}

To simulate regulatory noise, we allowed the fold-change parameters $\lambda_{Y_i}$ to vary stochastically over time rather than remaining fixed at their RACIPE-sampled value, using a periodic update applied every $dt = 0.01$ (a.u.) during ODE integration. Three noise modes were implemented, differing in how each update depends on the parameter's current value:
\begin{align}
\text{Additive:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(t) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2), \\
\text{Multiplicative:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(t)\left(1 + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2)\right), \\
\text{Stationary:} \quad & \lambda(t+dt) = \lambda(0) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2),
\end{align}
where $\sigma_{\text{eff}} = \sigma \cdot \lambda_{\max}$, with $\lambda_{\max}$ the upper bound of the relevant fold-change (inhibitory: $\lambda_{\max} = 1$, so $\sigma_{\text{eff}} \approx \sigma$; activatory: $\lambda_{\max} = 100$), so that a given nominal noise level $\sigma$ corresponds to a comparable proportion of each parameter's admissible range regardless of whether it governs activation or inhibition. After each update, $\lambda$ was clamped to its biologically admissible bounds ($[0.001, 0.999]$ for inhibitory, $[1.001, \lambda_{\max}]$ for activatory parameters). Noise levels $\sigma \in \{0, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}$ were tested for each noise mode and network.

\subsection{Stochastic simulation and mean residence time}

For each selected parameter set and noise level, 25 independent stochastic trajectories were simulated, each initialised from an independently drawn initial condition $u_i(0) = U(0,1) \times (g_i/d_i)$ for every node $i$ (i.e.\ uniformly between zero and that node's maximal attainable expression). Trajectories were integrated over $t \in [0, 1000]$ (a.u.) using the Tsit5 adaptive-step solver (relative tolerance $10^{-4}$, absolute tolerance $10^{-5}$). Each trajectory was discretized into a sequence of Boolean node states using the RACIPE-derived thresholds, and the first 30\% of each trajectory was discarded as transient. Mean residence time (MRT) for a given discrete state was computed as the fraction of the remaining trajectory (across all 25 stochastic realizations) spent in that state.

\subsection{Deterministic $\lambda$-resampling comparison}

To test whether a given noise mode's effect on state occupancy could be explained purely by the stationary distribution of $\lambda$ it induces, rather than by any dynamical (non-adiabatic) contribution, we compared stochastic MRT against a deterministic control: for each parameter set and noise level, $\lambda$ values were resampled from the empirical stationary distribution generated by that noise mode (via \texttt{get\_lambda\_dist}), and the corresponding deterministic steady state was solved; this was repeated over $\text{DET\_ITERS} = 10$ resampled draws per parameter set, and the resulting basin-occupancy fractions (MRT$_{\text{det}}$) were compared directly against the stochastic MRT from the same parameter set and noise level.


\subsection{Boolean simulations of large cell-fate networks}

For larger networks, we used Boolean modeling to simulate network dynamics, as opposed to the ODE models of RACIPE. We used a threshold-based Boolean formalism \cite{FontClos2018}, inspired by the Ising model of spin-glass systems. A network is defined by $N$ nodes, $E$ edges, and a topology, i.e., the arrangement of $E$ edges among $N$ nodes. The Ising model employs discretized expressions of nodes, with $s_i \in \{-1, 1\}$; $i \in 1, \ldots, N$, where $s_i = 1$ and $s_i = -1$ represent an active and inactive node, respectively. A state at any time $t$ is the set of expression levels of all nodes, $S(t) = \{s_i(t)\}$; $i \in 1, \ldots, N$.

The system state is updated in discrete time steps using an asymmetric adjacency matrix ($\adj$), which stores the nature of pairwise interactions between nodes. In a static network, activating and inhibiting edges carry edge weights of $+1$ or $-1$, respectively, with $0$ indicating no interaction. The expression level of node $i$ at time $t$ is given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bool}
    s_i(t) = 
\begin{cases}
    +1, & \text{if } \sum_j \adj_{ij} s_j(t-1) > 0 \\
    -1, & \text{if } \sum_j \adj_{ij} s_j(t-1) < 0 \\
    s_i(t-1), & \text{if } \sum_j \adj_{ij} s_j(t-1) = 0
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\sum_j \adj_{ij} s_j$ represents the net regulatory input to node $i$. A steady state is defined as a state $S(t)$ satisfying $S(t+1) = S(t)$.

\subsection{Large network metrics}

\subsubsection{Influence matrix}
The influence matrix is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    \inm_{l_\mathrm{max}} \equiv \inm = \frac{\sum_{l=1}^{l_\mathrm{max}} \frac{\adj^l}{\adj_{\mathrm{max}}^l}}{l_\mathrm{max}},
\end{equation}
where $l$ is the path length, representing a path of $l$ edges connecting a pair of nodes, and $l_\mathrm{max} = 10$ is the maximum path length considered. The influence matrix captures the net bidirectional effect between a pair of nodes mediated by all paths of length $l \in \{1, \ldots, l_\mathrm{max}\}$. $\adj_{\mathrm{max}}^l$ is derived from $\adj^l$ by setting all non-zero entries to $1$, and the division $\frac{\adj^l}{\adj_{\mathrm{max}}^l}$ is element-wise.

\subsubsection{Teams}
The wild-type influence matrices of the biological networks considered in this study are displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:5S1}. Each network can be partitioned into two ``teams'' of nodes, defined such that the influence between any pair of nodes is positive if they belong to the same team and negative otherwise. The team strength of a two-teamed network is defined as:
\begin{align} \label{eq:ts_def}
    \ts_{kl} &= \frac{\sum_{i \in T_k, j \in T_l} \inm_{ij}}{n_{kl}}, \quad k, l \in \{1, 2\} \\
    \ts &= \frac{\sum_{k,l \in \{1,2\}} |\ts_{kl}|}{4},
\end{align}
where $T_k$ is the $k$-th team.

\noindent\textbf{\textit{Team expression}}\label{text:team_exp}: For a given state $S$, team expression is defined as the fraction of nodes belonging to a team that are active (i.e., $s_i = 1$).

% \paragraph{Artificial networks} For artificial networks, the team identities are decided before generating the networks, and they are used directly without requiring the calculation of influence matrix.

\noindent\textbf{\textit{Nature of stable states}}: Stable states of two-team networks are classified as either dominant (most frequent) or hybrid (non-dominant). Due to the bimodal phenotypic distribution characteristic of toggle switch-like networks, dominant states are those in which only one team is active, i.e., states with team expressions of $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$.

\subsection{Stochastic simulation of large networks}

\subsubsection{Inducing noise}\label{sssec:noise_def}

In biological networks, the strengths of regulatory interactions are subject to temporal variation arising from fluctuations in protein concentrations, post-translational modifications, and other molecular sources. We model this regulatory noise by perturbing the edge weights of the adjacency matrix over time. Three noise modes are implemented, mirroring those used in the ODE-based simulations of small motifs (Section~\ref{sec:RACIPE}):

\paragraph{Additive (state-independent) noise}
At each time step, a perturbation drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ is added to each edge weight independently of its current value:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{EW}(t) = \mathrm{EW}(t-1) + \delta, \quad \delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Edge weights are clipped to $[-1, 1]$ after each update to preserve the biological interpretation of interaction strengths.

\paragraph{Multiplicative (state-dependent) noise}
At each time step, each edge weight is scaled by a factor drawn from $\mathcal{N}(1, \sigma^2)$, so that the magnitude of the perturbation scales with the current edge weight:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{EW}(t) = \mathrm{EW}(t-1) \cdot (1 + \delta), \quad \delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stationary (mean-reverting) noise}
At each time step, each edge weight is redrawn as its wild-type value plus an independent perturbation, so that the weight fluctuates around its nominal value without drift:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{EW}(t) = \mathrm{EW}_0 + \delta, \quad \delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{EW}_0$ denotes the wild-type edge weight. Unlike the additive mode, this formulation prevents long-range drift in parameter values and models a situation where molecular fluctuations are transient and the nominal interaction strength is effectively restored between perturbation events.

In all three modes, $\sigma^2 = 0.01$ is used as the noise variance. Each biological network is simulated for $1000$ trajectories of $10{,}000$ time steps each; artificial networks are simulated for $100$ trajectories of $1{,}000$ time steps each.

\subsubsection{Method of state update}
In practice, the system can be updated in two ways \cite{garg2008synchronous, schwab2020concepts}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synchronous update}: At each time step, every node is updated simultaneously using Eq.~\ref{eq:bool}.
    \item \textbf{Asynchronous update}: At each time step, a single randomly chosen node is updated using Eq.~\ref{eq:bool}.
\end{itemize}
While synchronous updates are computationally efficient, they can produce limit cycles from which the system cannot escape. Asynchronous updates are less efficient but avoid limit cycles and implicitly capture reaction noise by assuming that only one regulatory event occurs per time step. To combine the benefits of both schemes, we check for steady states synchronously every $200$ time steps while updating the network asynchronously. For continuous perturbation simulations, synchronous updates are used throughout, as persistent perturbations make entrapment in limit cycles unlikely.

\subsubsection{Initial conditions}

Peripheral nodes — those involved in interactions without feedback — are excluded from the analysis, as the focus of this study is on teaming behaviour arising from mutual regulatory effects. Initial conditions are drawn from the terminal states and the most dominant hybrid states identified in the unperturbed network's steady-state distribution.
